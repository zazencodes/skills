# C. Language

1. Compare phrases with their actual meaning: inflated verbs, staged contrasts, uniform triads, stock transitions, and synonym churn.
2. Replace wording that obscures or exaggerates. Prefer basic “is” and “has” when they express the relationship accurately.
3. Check the examples' exceptions before changing a construction merely because it appears on a watchlist.
4. Preserve technical terms and the claim's strength; recheck fidelity before D.

## Examples and boundaries

**Given:** A library has four reading rooms.
**Defective:** “The library serves as a vibrant intellectual hub, boasting four distinct reading spaces.”
**Revision:** “The library has four reading rooms.”
**Why:** The added language supplies praise, while “spaces” loses the original precision.
**Preserve:** “The school serves as an evacuation shelter during floods” expresses a temporary function. Replacing it with “is a shelter” would change the meaning.

**Given:** An export tool saves a CSV file. No misconception or competing claim is supplied.
**Defective:** “This isn't just an export. It's a revolution in how teams work.”
**Revision:** “The tool saves a CSV file.”
**Why:** The contrast stages a revelation and invents impact.
**Preserve:** “The command deletes the local copy, not the remote backup” distinguishes two real outcomes that the reader needs to understand. Contrasts can be necessary.

**Given:** Supported formats are CSV, JSON, and Markdown.
**Defective:** “Unlock seamless, powerful, transformative exports.”
**Revision:** “Export CSV, JSON, and Markdown.”
**Why:** The factual three-item list matters. The adjective trio adds no supported information.
**Preserve:** Rhetorical repetition or a triad can work in a speech. Judge its contribution and the author's voice; never force lists to have two or four items.

Keep terminology stable: do not alternate “cache,” “store,” and “memory layer” merely for variety. A literal underscore, a necessary transition, a justified superlative, or a familiar formal term needs no replacement. Inspect patterns in context; word frequency and model-era vocabulary lists are not universal bans. A title such as “List of local parks” need not be defined as a real-world entity in the opening sentence.
