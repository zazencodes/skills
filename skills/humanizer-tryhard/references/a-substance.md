# A. Substance

Compare each assertion with the supplied facts before polishing its language.

1. Identify the concrete assertion. Separate an event from claims about its importance, consequences, motivations, or symbolism.
2. Examine trailing “-ing” clauses and vague associations. Ask what relationship is actually supported.
3. Remove unsupported interpretation. Use available specifics; do not invent specifics to replace abstractions.
4. Check the revision against the factual ledger before moving to B.

## Examples and boundaries

**Given:** The archive opened in 1989 and holds 4,000 maps.
**Defective:** “Opened in 1989, the archive marked a pivotal cultural shift, underscoring the city's commitment to preserving its identity.”
**Revision:** “The archive opened in 1989 and holds 4,000 maps.”
**Why:** Opening date and collection size support a useful description. Cultural significance and civic motivation need separate evidence.
**Preserve:** If an identified historian actually argues that the opening changed access to local records, retain that attributed argument with its evidence. Interpretation is allowed when supported and appropriate to the genre.

**Given:** Mara taught chemistry at North College from 2018 to 2021.
**Defective:** “Mara was associated with science education at North College, contributing to academic excellence.”
**Revision:** “Mara taught chemistry at North College from 2018 to 2021.”
**Why:** The vague connection obscures the known role; academic excellence adds an unsupported result.
**Preserve:** “Associated with” can be accurate when the evidence establishes an association but cannot establish a specific role. Never strengthen uncertain evidence to make prose sharper.

**Given:** A label put a singer on a catalogue cover.
**Defective:** “The cover reflected the image the label wanted to promote.”
**Revision:** State that the singer appeared on the cover.
**Why:** Even restrained wording can invent intent. A marketing brief could support the interpretation; the cover alone cannot.

Source coverage: Content; significance and legacy; superficial analyses; vague connections; historical promotional language. Generic environmental, cultural, or future-impact claims require the same scrutiny. Historical detection patterns remain useful editorial questions, not proof of authorship.
