# B. Evidence

1. Match factual claims and attributed opinions to their actual supporting material.
2. Check whose view it is, how many sources support it, and whether qualifications survived.
3. Check citations for existence, relevance, and enough detail to locate the passage. A resolving URL or valid identifier alone is insufficient.
4. Remove invented consensus and prestige claims; recheck fidelity before C.

## Examples and boundaries

**Given:** One named reviewer calls a release difficult to configure.
**Defective:** “Experts agree that the release has serious usability problems.”
**Revision:** “Reviewer Lena Ortiz found the release difficult to configure.”
**Why:** One review supports one attributed assessment, not consensus or a broader defect.
**Preserve:** A supplied survey of 200 reviewers can support an aggregate claim, within the survey's scope. Do not mechanically turn every finding into an individual opinion.

**Given:** A newspaper reports that a venue opened in June.
**Defective:** “The venue's significance is documented by independent national media coverage.”
**Revision:** “The venue opened in June.” Attach the source if the format calls for citations.
**Why:** Describe the fact rather than praising the existence or category of coverage. Primary records and directories often support plain facts without repeated “records identify” framing.
**Preserve:** Attribution matters for disputed facts, self-reported claims, or an analysis of media coverage itself. Do not erase it for brevity.

**Defective:** A DOI resolves to a real paper, but the paper studies a different treatment from the sentence citing it.
**Repair:** Flag the mismatch, verify a suitable source, or remove the unsupported assertion. Never invent a replacement DOI, page number, quotation, or citation.
**Preserve:** A broken link may reflect link rot, access restrictions, or a typo. A book without a URL may still be valid. Report the actual verification limit without claiming fabrication or that no evidence exists.

Check exact quotations, source counts, source dates, DOI/ISBN metadata, book pages, and whether references are used and defined where needed. Separate a source's stated finding from your inference. Avoid skewing coverage toward familiar languages or regions when the supplied evidence is broader.

Source coverage: notability/media coverage; identification by sources; online presence; vague attribution; Citations and its subsections; biases in content. Tracking parameters may reveal a retrieval tool, not who wrote the prose; preserve working links and required attribution.
