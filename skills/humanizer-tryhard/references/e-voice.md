# E. Voice and cadence

1. Compare the revised copy with available author samples and the intended genre.
2. Read the complete piece aloud or simulate its spoken cadence. Inspect repeated openings, forced symmetry, abrupt register shifts, and mechanically short sentences.
3. Adjust syntax to fit the meaning. Preserve purposeful quirks, repetition, humor, emphasis, and legitimate uncertainty.
4. Recheck facts and stance before F. Without samples, follow the brief conservatively; do not claim a precise voice match.

## Examples and boundaries

**Given author wording:** “I waited three hours. Three hours, for a form they already had.”
**Overedited:** “The prolonged administrative delay caused significant frustration.”
**Repair:** Keep the original wording when it suits the piece.
**Why:** The repetition carries the author's supplied frustration. Abstract formality erases it.
**Boundary:** Do not invent the wait or frustration to make an otherwise factual notice feel personal.

**Mechanical revision:** “The build failed. We checked the logs. A token had expired. We renewed it. The build passed.”
**Possible revision:** “The build failed because a token had expired. We renewed it and the build passed.”
**Why:** Sentence structure now follows the causal relationship.
**Preserve:** Short sentences may be deliberate in a dramatic narrative. Longer sentences, dashes, semicolons, and parentheses may be natural too; neither a length quota nor punctuation avoidance produces voice.

**Given:** “The result may depend on temperature.”
**Overconfident edit:** “Temperature determines the result.”
**Repair:** Preserve “may” unless the evidence supports certainty.
**Why:** Uncertainty is part of the assertion, not removable weakness.

Perfect grammar, formality, mixed casual/technical registers, and isolated transitions are compatible with human writing. Keep strong or superlative statements when supported. Avoid random slang, typos, forced jokes, invented anecdotes, or arbitrary sentence variation. Typography and list styles should suit the destination and author; style alone does not prove authorship. Historical punctuation patterns change across models and periods.

Source coverage: Style; pronounced shifts; differences between LLMs; human syntax; ineffective indicators; historical em dashes and lexical variation. Provenance and the ability to explain choices concern authorship assessment, not instructions to manufacture a human persona.
